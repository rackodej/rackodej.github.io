Impressions

Overall, the exam is neither easy nor hard, but it’s extensive in both practical and theoretical knowledge. I’d rate it at a solid mid-level. The biggest snag for 2025 is the requirement to hand-write code, without an LLM, for the final one-shot exploits.
Given what modern local models can do, this requirement feels debatable: it tests muscle memory and familiarity with common patterns more than analysis and solution design. I had to adapt and dust off some old skills.

Main complaints about the infrastructure:

  1. You can’t copy code out. Train yourself to keep the app context in your head and via screenshots.
  2. FPS in the Linux RDP session is very low — +50 to the suffering.
  3. X11 forwarding turned out to be completely dead: VS Code couldn’t even fully load.

🗓️ A year before the exam

I planned to do all the labs at least once or twice a week.
Naturally, that didn’t really happen.

⏳ A month before the exam

I read the theory cover to cover. Very little was new: the material is dull, poorly structured, and the logic of presentation jumps around. On top of that, the course turned out to be incomplete—there were topics on the exam that weren’t in the text at all.

⏰ Two hours before the exam

Before you start, check:

  1. Your Kali VM is working, Ctrl+C/V work on the host system, and files copy over.
  2. Your webcam works. My Insta360 didn’t work “out of the box” on Ubuntu—I had to urgently grab a laptop. Burp ate all the RAM, so I had to quickly sort out UVC/UAC. By the way, Burp is the only program that consumes more memory than Chrome.
  3. The router had XRAY+Reality configured. Because of this, the connection to the exam network wouldn’t establish. I had to switch to the ISP network. Downside: some sites are blocked—you can’t read the info you need.
  4. Passport (international): you’ll be asked to show it to the camera, as well as the room. No lighting requirements.

🛠️ The exam

👀 Proctoring

Proctoring itself is a web page where all your screens are streamed (I had three monitors). The UI/UX is slapped together, so be ready to be asked to restart screen sharing repeatedly. At the start of the exam, they’ll ask you to run a couple of typical commands on the system and upload the output to their pastebin. They do this once for the entire exam. After I moved to another machine, they didn’t request the output again.
On the second day, I took short breaks every hour. You just write in the chat that you need a break and go handle your stuff.

🔓 Task #1

The exam started at 21:00; ~48 hours in total are given. That turned out to be great for me: you can skim the code of the first task, outline possible vectors, go to sleep, and in the morning—fresh—start implementing the plan.
Impression-wise, the split is: the first task is comprehensive, while the second is solved rather bluntly, without finesse or multithreaded programming.

After waking up, I got to work: coding, searching, writing—and by 18:00 it turned out my solution didn’t work. Time and energy wasted;
You’re repeating the year. Should’ve solved the machines. I had to switch to a more obvious approach. By 23:00 I had a full solution, and closer to 02:00 I finished the one-shot exploit. Went to sleep. By the end of Day 1, one machine (50 points) was fully solved. You need 85 points minimum. I hadn’t even looked at the second task yet.

🔓 Task #2

Woke up at 9:30. After carefully reading the description, it became clear where to go.
The description may hint at which vectors to look for in general.
After obtaining admin rights, the RCE came even easier: the source code + settings made the spot obvious. It was 16:00. All four flags were collected across the two tasks (per task: 35 for admin rights and 15 for RCE). Two hours later I wrote the one-shot exploit.

📝 The report

I finished the exam two hours before the official end. If you had lab/connection issues, you can try to negotiate an extension.

I decided not to put off the report and wrote it that same evening—this may have saved my exam. The next morning the VM (VirtualBox; VMWare never worked) hard-hung. All console and Burp material was lost.

The report took another five hours. Before closing the exam early, I recommend taking your exploits and re-capturing all key steps with screenshots along the exploitation path so you won’t have to describe gaps in the evidence “in words”.

The result arrives by email within a day; they don’t disclose the exact number of points credited.

Try to account for low-probability events: power outage, internet loss, random reboot.

The tasks don’t require superhuman effort. Don’t forget to eat, sleep well, and take walks. There’s plenty of time.

⭐ Subjective rating: 6.5/10
